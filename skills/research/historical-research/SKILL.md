# Historical Research

Systematic research workflow for historical studies using source criticism and scholarly methodology.

## When to use
- Historical events, periods, or civilizations
- Biographical research (political, intellectual, cultural figures)
- Historiography: how history is written across periods
- Source criticism: evaluating authenticity, bias, context
- Comparative history across regions/cultures
- Archaeological and material evidence integration
- Dating, chronology, and timeline verification
- Historical cause/effect analysis

## Source hierarchy
1. **Primary sources** (highest value):
   - Contemporary documents, inscriptions, coins
   - Eyewitness accounts, chronicles, court records
   - Archaeological evidence: artifacts, architecture, stratigraphy
2. **Secondary scholarly sources:**
   - Peer-reviewed monographs from academic presses
   - University press publications (Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, etc.)
   - Encyclopaedia of Islam, Brill, Oxford Classical Dictionary
3. **Tertiary (background/reference):**
   - Encyclopaedia Britannica, Oxford Reference
   - Academic bibliographies and literature reviews
4. **Cross-verification required for:**
   - Oral histories, folk traditions, popular narratives
   - Nationalist/ideological historiography
   - Single-source claims regardless of author fame

## Core methodology
### Source criticism (Garraghan-Delanglez model)
1. **When** was the source produced?
2. **Where** was it produced?
3. **By whom** — established authorship?
4. **From what** pre-existing material?
5. **Original form** preserved or transmitted?
6. **Evidential value/credibility** of its contents?

### Reliability principles
1. Independently created sources agreeing = higher credibility
2. Eyewitness > secondhand > hearsay > later tradition
3. Proximity to event increases accuracy
4. Bias/tendency must be identified and minimized
5. Corroboration across traditions strengthens claims
6. Absence of independent confirmation weakens single-source claims

### Historical-critical steps
1. External criticism: establish date, place, authorship, integrity
2. Internal criticism: evaluate style, language, consistency, bias
3. Authenticity criteria: external (manuscript tradition) and internal (linguistic)
4. Contextualization: place source within social/political/intellectual context
5. Synthesis: weigh evidence, acknowledge uncertainties, avoid overstatement

## Output rules
- Always specify source type: primary/secondary, date, author
- Distinguish established fact ( consensus) from probable from speculative
- Note conflicting interpretations when present
- Include chronological boundaries explicitly
- Add geographic/cultural context for non-Western history
- Use modern scholarly terminology; avoid anachronisms
- Present historiographical debates fairly

## Anti-patterns
- Presenting single-source narrative as definitive fact
- Ignoring chronological gaps or evidence absences
- Retrojecting modern values onto past actors/contexts
- conflating legend/myth with historical core without distinction
- Cherry-picking sources that support pre-formed conclusion
- Overstating archaeological evidence beyond what it actually shows

## Session-proven fallback sources
- Wikipedia for **orientation only** — always verify citations
- JSTOR open access / Project MUSE
- Academia.edu preprints with caution
- Google Books previews for older scholarship
- Digital libraries: Internet Archive, Gallica, DAR
- Specialized: Perseus (ancient), Aluka (Islamic Africa), Stanford's China
- Shamela web: https://shamela.ws — classical Arabic books with search
- Sunnah.com hadith search — useful for dating/seerah references

## Language handling
- Original language preferred for primary source excerpts
- Transliteration standard: Arabic (ISO 233), Chinese (Pinyin)
- Translations clearly labeled with translator/date
- Maintain RTL for Arabic; standard LTR for historical Latin/Greek
